# Career advocacy — getting seen, and getting things through

<!-- REVIEW-BY: 2027-01-31 -->
**Verified 2026-07-29.** Volatile figures live in `career-coach/references/market-snapshot-2026-06.md`.

## 0. The term, because it matters

**This is not "social engineering".** In this library and in security generally, that term means
*deceiving a person into breaching a control* — `llm-security` and `threat-modeling` use it that way,
and it names an attack. Borrowing it for career work imports the wrong instincts.

**The right names are organisational influence, internal advocacy and informal networks** — and they
are ordinary, legitimate professional skills. Work does not speak for itself, decisions are made by
people, and people decide about what they have seen and understood.

**The line, and it is a bright one:**

| Influence | Manipulation |
|---|---|
| The other person still agrees **after** seeing your full reasoning | Depends on them *not* seeing it |
| Serves an outcome they can also want | Serves only you, dressed as mutual |
| Information is offered | Information is withheld or timed to prevent scrutiny |
| Survives being described out loud | Cannot be described out loud |

**The working test: could you say what you are doing, to the person you are doing it to, without the
tactic failing?** Pre-briefing a stakeholder passes — you can say "I wanted your view before Thursday
so I'm not springing it on you." Timing a proposal so a known objector is on leave does not.

**Manipulation buys one decision and costs the relationship.** In an organisation you may be in for
years, and a senior market small enough that people share notes, that is a bad trade even before it
is a wrong one.

## 1. The insight everything else follows from

**Decisions are not made in the meeting. They are made in the hours and days before it, and the
meeting documents a decision that has largely already happened.**

People form views while reading, while thinking, while talking to a colleague they trust. By the time
they are sitting in the room with your deck on screen, most of them arrived with a position.

**So a proposal that first meets its audience in the meeting is already losing**, however good it is.
Everything in §2 follows from taking that literally.

## 2. Pre-wiring a decision

The consulting-firm practice, and it is not exotic: **before a recommendation goes to a committee,
every decision-maker has already seen it individually and their position is known.**

1. **Separate who *decides* from who can *veto*.** They are rarely the same people, and a veto is
   almost always exercised by someone who was surprised. Map both (`career-leadership` §4).
2. **See each of them one-to-one first.** Ten minutes is enough. The purpose is not to present —
   it is to *hear their objection while it is still cheap*.
3. **Send a short pre-read.** People process in writing before they judge in a room, and it is the
   only way to reach the ones who will not meet you.
4. **Change the proposal where the objection is right.** Pre-wiring that never alters anything is a
   sales tour, and people can tell. **The visible incorporation of someone's point is what converts
   them into a supporter** — they now have authorship.
5. **Know where every attendee stands before you walk in.** If you cannot name each person's
   position, you are not ready; that is a reason to move the meeting, not to hope.
6. **Give a supporter something specific to say.** "Would you make the delivery-risk point? It lands
   better from you." Advocates need a line, not just goodwill.

**Objections raised in private are cheap; the same objection in the room is expensive**, because now
it is public, the objector is committed to it, and reversing costs them face.

**Where this becomes factionalising:** if the pre-wires would look like lobbying against a person
rather than toward a decision, you are over the line in §0. The tell is whether you would be
comfortable if everyone compared notes — because on a decision that matters, they will.

## 3. Getting the demo, deck or proposal through the door

- **Lead with their problem, not your solution.** The first line decides whether the rest is read.
  "Our reconciliation takes six days and closes late every month" opens; "I've built an AI tool"
  does not.
- **Have a one-paragraph version, a one-slide version and the full version**, and lead with the
  smallest. Senior attention is granted in increments, and the full deck is what you send *after*
  someone asks.
- **Make the ask explicit and small.** "Twenty minutes with you and the ops lead" beats "let me know
  your thoughts" — a vague ask converts to nothing because nobody knows what agreeing means.
- **Who forwards it matters more than what it says.** A proposal arriving from a respected internal
  sender is read; the same document arriving cold is queued. **Ask someone to forward it**, and give
  them the sentence to put above it.
- **Time it to the cycle.** Budget planning, quarterly planning and post-incident windows are when
  proposals get funded; three weeks after the budget closed is a good idea nobody can act on.
- **Show it working.** A three-minute demo of something real outperforms twenty slides about
  something planned, and it converts sceptics that argument does not.
- **Name the cost, the risk and what you need.** Proposals that omit these read as unserious, and the
  first question exposes it anyway.

**Volunteer for the all-hands slot, the brown-bag, the demo day.** These are consistently
under-subscribed, they reach exactly the sideways-and-upward audience that matters, and the barrier
is willingness rather than permission.

## 4. Internal channels — Teams, Slack, the intranet

**Under hybrid working, proximity bias is real and measurable**, and the counter is not being in the
office more. **It is sharing work beyond your immediate team, in writing, routinely** — that
correlates with promotion among otherwise equally productive people.

- **Post in channels, not DMs.** A DM reaches one person once; a channel post is visible, searchable
  and forwardable. Same effort, different half-life.
- **Write the update people can act on.** What changed · what it means for them · what you need.
  Status updates with no "so what" train people to skip you.
- **Be useful in *other* people's channels.** Answering a question well in another team's channel is
  the fastest cross-organisation reputation there is, and almost nobody does it deliberately.
- **Convert the thread into an artefact.** A Teams answer disappears; the same content as a page
  people link to keeps working (`career-internal-visibility` §7).
- **Show up in the ambient moments.** Turn the camera on for the meeting where decisions get made;
  join the informal channel; say the useful thing in the pre-meeting chatter. Presence at those is
  disproportionately what "he/she is around" is built from.
- **Do not perform activity.** Constant posting with low signal is visible as noise and costs
  credibility. **One substantial thing a week beats five thin ones.**

## 5. Business and industry social events

**Set a small, specific goal: three real conversations.** "Work the room" produces a pocket of cards
and no relationships. Depth converts; breadth does not.

- **Prepare, which beats extroversion.** Check the attendee list beforehand — most events publish one
  or run an app — and pick three people worth meeting and why. Preparation is available to everyone;
  natural sociability is not.
- **If you find these draining, your strengths are the right ones anyway.** Listening properly and
  asking a genuinely good question is what people remember; talking most is not. **Nobody remembers
  the person who talked the longest.**
- **Have a plain-language answer to "what do you do".** One sentence, no jargon, ending somewhere the
  other person can pick up.
- **Ask about their problems and listen.** It is more useful than pitching and it is what makes the
  follow-up natural.
- **Give something before you need something** — an introduction, a reference, an answer.
- **Follow up within 24–48 hours, referencing something specific from the conversation.** This is the
  step that separates a contact from a memory, and most people skip it entirely.
- **Build the next contact into this one.** "I'll send that over — shall we talk again after your
  launch?" A relationship is several interactions; one is a business card.

**Internal social events are work, and worth treating as such.** They are where you meet the people
two levels up and one department across who otherwise never see you — which is exactly the audience
that decides things (`career-internal-visibility` §1).

## 6. External platforms, aimed inward

To reach people inside organisations you want to work with, the mechanics are in
`career-coach/references/linkedin-mechanics.md`. The two moves that matter here:

- **Comment substantively on the right people's posts.** Being *replied to* by someone widely read
  puts you in front of their audience — far more reach than posting to your own network, and it
  reaches the author too.
- **Post consistently on the target topic**, because distribution is interest-based and topical
  consistency compounds into being one of the people associated with it.

**Warm beats cold, every time.** A message referencing a shared connection, a talk you both attended
or something they wrote gets a reply; a generic outreach does not.

## 7. Making the ask

Most advocacy fails at the last step, because nobody was actually asked for anything.

- **Specific.** "Would you sponsor this at the platform review on the 14th?" not "any support
  welcome".
- **Small and bounded.** People say yes to a defined cost. Open-ended requests get deferred, and
  deferred is a no with better manners.
- **Easy to act on.** Attach the thing, draft the forwarding line, propose the time.
- **Give a deadline and a reason for it.** "Before Thursday, because that is when the slate closes."
- **Make refusal cheap.** "Entirely fine if not" gets more yeses than pressure, and it protects the
  relationship for the next ask.
- **Then close the loop.** Tell them what happened. People who never hear the outcome do not help
  twice.

## 8. Where this backfires

- **Visibility with nothing behind it.** Being known for being visible is a short-lived and damaging
  reputation. **Advocacy amplifies work; it does not substitute for it**, and the gap always shows.
- **Appearing only when you need something.** Transactional contact is recognised instantly and
  remembered.
- **Over-claiming shared credit** in a public channel where the people who did the work can see it.
- **Pre-wiring that becomes lobbying**, or a pre-wire framed differently to different people —
  inconsistency between rooms is the most reliably fatal error here, and people do compare.
- **Volume as strategy** — posting constantly, attending everything, following up too hard.

## 9. Anti-patterns

- **Calling this "social engineering"** and importing the instincts of an attack.
- **Letting a proposal meet its audience for the first time in the meeting.**
- **Pre-wiring without changing anything** — a sales tour, and it is transparent.
- **Not knowing where each attendee stands** before walking in.
- **Timing a decision around an objector's absence.**
- **Leading with your solution** instead of their problem.
- **Sending the full deck first.**
- **A vague ask** — nobody knows what agreeing to it means.
- **DMs where a channel post would do.**
- **Status updates with no "so what".**
- **Collecting contacts** instead of having three real conversations.
- **Skipping the 24–48 hour follow-up**, which is where the value was.
- **Never closing the loop** with someone who helped.
- **Visibility exceeding substance.**
