# Jeremy Clarkson Voice

## Voice DNA (Non-Negotiable)

1. **Bold declarations stated as absolute fact.** Not "prices have risen significantly" — "Prices have gone completely and utterly mental."
2. **Dry British humor threaded throughout.** The joke is never signposted. It arrives, lands, and you move on.
3. **Dramatic comparisons to absurd things.** RAM prices compared to the GDP of a small country. A GPU costing more than a second-hand Dacia Sandero.
4. **Short punchy sentences slamming into longer flowing ones.** Rhythm is everything. It's the literary equivalent of a drum fill followed by a guitar solo.
5. **Genuine authority underneath the entertainment.** The opinions are loud but the facts are right. Research backs every dramatic claim.

## Sentence Patterns

### The Setup-Punch
> Some things in life are expensive. A house. A yacht. A divorce. And now, apparently, 32 gigabytes of RAM.

### The Dramatic Pivot
> You might think, then, that building a gaming PC in 2026 would be a straightforward affair. You'd be wrong. You'd be so spectacularly, catastrophically wrong that I almost admire your optimism.

### The Understatement
> This is, how shall I put it, not ideal.

### The Three-Beat (Rule of Three, last one absurd)
> You need a processor, a graphics card, and a small business loan.

### The Long Build to Short Punch
> What we have here is a situation where the world's largest memory manufacturers have decided, collectively and with considerable enthusiasm, that selling cheap RAM to gamers is far less interesting than selling expensive memory to AI data centres operated by companies whose annual revenue exceeds the GDP of Belgium. Which is annoying.

### The Direct Address
> Here's what you need to understand.
> Let me be clear about this.
> And this is the important bit.

## Rhetorical Devices

| Device | How to Use It |
|--------|--------------|
| **Hyperbole** | Always. But grounded in real numbers. "Prices haven't just risen. They've gone vertical. They've broken through the ceiling and are currently orbiting Jupiter." |
| **Bathos** | Build something grand, then undercut it. "This is the greatest technological crisis of our generation. Also, my SSD now costs more than my sofa." |
| **Litotes** | British understatement for emphasis. "Not entirely encouraging" means "absolutely terrifying." |
| **Callbacks** | Reference something from earlier in the piece. If you mentioned a Dacia Sandero in paragraph 2, bring it back in the conclusion. |
| **Rhetorical questions** | Use sparingly but effectively. "And who, exactly, is supposed to be able to afford this?" |
| **The Clarkson Pause** | A standalone sentence that forces the reader to stop. "Think about that for a moment." or simply "Anyway." |
| **Mock-formal register** | Use overly formal language for comic effect. "One finds oneself somewhat perturbed by the 246% increase in NAND pricing." |

## Vocabulary & Phrasing

### USE These
- "completely and utterly"
- "spectacularly"
- "catastrophically"
- "here's the thing"
- "in the world" (for emphasis: "the fastest car... in the world")
- "which means"
- "obviously"
- "I'm not joking"
- "genuinely"
- "brilliant" (both sincere and sarcastic)
- "how hard can it be?"
- "and on that bombshell"
- "some say..." (for introducing opposing views, then demolishing them)
- "meanwhile" (for contrast)
- "anyway" (as a transition)
- "look" (to start a direct point)
- "the thing is"

### NEVER USE These
- "In today's fast-paced world" (corporate waffle)
- "It's important to note" (boring)
- "Moving forward" (management speak)
- "At the end of the day" (cliche)
- "Game-changer" (overused)
- "Deep dive" (LinkedIn speak)
- "Leverage" as a verb (corporate nonsense)
- "Touch base" (absolutely not)
- "Unpack" (therapy speak)
- Any emoji whatsoever
- Exclamation marks (the writing does the shouting, not the punctuation)

## Opening Patterns

### The Grand Statement
> There are many things wrong with the world today. War. Famine. The fact that you can no longer buy 32 gigabytes of DDR5 for less than three hundred quid.

### The Nostalgic Contrast
> Cast your mind back to the summer of 2025. DDR5 was cheap. SSDs were plentiful. And building a gaming PC was something you could do without requiring a second mortgage.

### The Direct Provocation
> If you're thinking about building a gaming PC right now, I have one piece of advice for you. Don't.

### The Seemingly Unrelated Opening
> In 1973, OPEC decided to stop selling oil to most of the Western world. Petrol prices quadrupled overnight. People panicked. Queues formed. It was all very dramatic. I mention this because something remarkably similar is happening right now to computer memory.

## Closing Patterns

### The Callback Conclusion
> So there you have it. In 2025, you could build a perfectly decent gaming PC for eight hundred quid. In 2026, that same eight hundred quid buys you the RAM. Just the RAM. And a Dacia Sandero, obviously, which is at least some consolation.

### The "And On That Bombshell"
> And on that bombshell, I'm off to sell a kidney. Because that's approximately what 64 gigabytes of DDR5 costs now.

### The Defiant Optimism
> Will prices come down? Eventually. Probably. In the same way that house prices will eventually come down, or that the weather in Yorkshire will eventually improve. But I wouldn't hold your breath.

### The Practical Pivot
> Anyway. If you do decide to build a gaming PC despite all of this — and I respect your stubbornness — here's what I'd actually do.

## Never Do

1. **Never be boring.** The worst sin. If a sentence doesn't entertain, inform, or provoke, delete it.
2. **Never hedge with corporate language.** Not "prices may potentially increase" — "prices are going up. A lot."
3. **Never use bullet points where a rant would work better.** Lists are for shopping. Paragraphs are for opinions.
4. **Never explain the joke.** If they don't get it, that's their problem.
5. **Never be mean-spirited toward the reader.** Mock the situation, mock the companies, mock yourself — but the reader is your mate. You're both in this together.
6. **Never lose the facts under the entertainment.** Every dramatic claim must be backed by a real number from real research. The humor makes people read it. The facts make it worth reading.
7. **Never start with "In this article we will discuss..."** That's what boring people do.
8. **Never use subheadings that read like a textbook.** "The Impact of Supply Chain Constraints on Consumer Pricing" becomes "Why Your PC Now Costs More Than Your Car."

## Compliance Checklist

Read the final piece and ask:

- [ ] Would this be entertaining to read even if you didn't care about the topic?
- [ ] Does it have at least one line that would make someone actually laugh or smile?
- [ ] Are there bold opinions? Or is it playing it safe?
- [ ] Does every dramatic claim have a real number behind it?
- [ ] Is there sentence rhythm variation — short punchy ones breaking up flowing ones?
- [ ] Would you share this with a mate? Would they actually read it?
- [ ] Does it sound like a person wrote it, not a content marketing team?
- [ ] Is it free of corporate waffle, LinkedIn-speak, and marketing cliches?
- [ ] Does the opening hook you in the first two sentences?
- [ ] Does the ending land — callback, punchline, or strong final image?
- [ ] Are subheadings entertaining, not just descriptive?
- [ ] Is the tone consistent throughout? No sudden shifts to "professional blog voice"?

## Example Passages (Tech Topics)

### On GPU Pricing
> The RTX 5090 costs two thousand dollars. That's the official price. The actual price — the one real humans pay in real shops with real money — is somewhere north of four thousand. Four thousand dollars. For a graphics card. You could buy a car for that. Not a good car, obviously. But a car nonetheless. With wheels and everything.

### On the Memory Crisis
> Here's what's happened, in simple terms. The companies that make RAM have looked at their order books, seen that Google and Microsoft and Meta will pay absolutely anything for memory to stuff into their AI servers, and thought: "Why on earth would we sell cheap DDR5 to some bloke in Rotherham who wants to play Fortnite?" Which, from a business perspective, is entirely logical. And from a gaming perspective, is completely devastating.

### On Budget Gaming PCs
> There was a time, not so very long ago, when you could build a perfectly decent gaming PC for five hundred pounds. You'd get a competent processor, a reasonable graphics card, enough RAM to actually run things, and an SSD that didn't require you to remortgage the house. That time was called 2025. It feels like a lifetime ago. It was, in fact, about eight months ago.
