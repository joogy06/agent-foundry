# How LinkedIn surfaces you — profile, content, connections, certifications

<!-- REVIEW-BY: 2027-01-31 -->
**Captured 2026-07-29.** Shared reference for the `career-*` family. Used by `career-positioning`

## 0. Read this before believing any number about LinkedIn

**Almost every specific statistic circulating about the LinkedIn algorithm comes from vendors selling
LinkedIn tools, not from LinkedIn.** "Verified skills rank 30% higher", "All-Star profiles appear in
40× more searches", "the headline is weighted 5×", "comments carry 15× the weight of likes" — these
are content marketing. They are repeated confidently across hundreds of pages, which is evidence of
copying, not of truth.

**LinkedIn does publish algorithm explanations through its own team and a few outlets that interview
them.** Prefer those. Treat the rest as folklore.

**This file therefore states MECHANISMS, which are stable and observable, and avoids multipliers.**
The mechanisms are enough to act on; the multipliers would only tell you how hard to push, and they
are not reliable enough to answer that.

## 1. Profile surfacing — you are matched, not browsed

**Recruiters do not scroll. They search, and an engine decides whether you appear at all.**

**Search is semantic, not keyword matching.** LinkedIn matches against a skills graph relating
skills, titles and industries, so it can connect "led model deployment" to a search for "MLOps"
without the literal phrase. This is the same shift ATS made — see `ai-hiring-funnel.md` — and it has
the same consequence:

**Stuffing keywords is counterproductive.** It reads as a poor semantic match and as inauthentic to
the human who eventually looks. **Describe real work in the field's normal vocabulary instead.**

What actually governs whether you surface:

| Element | Why it matters mechanically |
|---|---|
| **Headline** | Short, heavily weighted, and the only text shown in search results. It is your search snippet, not your job title |
| **About, first lines** | Truncated in preview — front-load what you want matched on |
| **Skills section** | **Structured data, directly filterable in Recruiter.** Prose cannot be filtered; a skill entry can |
| **Experience entries** | Where scope and seniority are inferred — titles alone are ambiguous across companies |
| **Completeness** | An empty field cannot match anything. Incompleteness is absence, not neutrality |
| **Location and open-to-work** | Hard filters. A wrong or missing location silently excludes you |

**The skills section is the most under-used lever**, because it is the only part of your profile that
is structured, filterable data. Populate it deliberately with the skills for the role you *want*, not
only the ones you have most of. Endorsements do appear to feed ranking; the honest statement is that
more, from credible people in the field, help — not that any specific count is a threshold.

**Open-to-work has two modes.** The recruiter-only setting is invisible to your network; the public
green banner is visible to everyone including your employer. **They are different decisions** and the
distinction is worth making deliberately rather than by accident.

## 2. Connections — degree affects both search and reach

**Your network shapes what recruiters see and who sees your posts.**

- Recruiters can see and filter by connection degree, and shared connections make outreach more
  likely — a 2nd-degree connection with a mutual colleague is a warmer result than a 3rd.
- **Connections in your target field matter more than connection count.** Being connected to 400
  relevant people beats 4,000 arbitrary ones for both search adjacency and content reach.
- **Content reaches 1st-degree connections first**, and prioritises those you interact with
  regularly. A network that never engages with you suppresses your reach regardless of size.

**Connect with intent, and engage before you need anything.** A dormant network is a list, not a
network — and its value shows up exactly when you can no longer build it in time.

## 3. Content distribution — what actually decides reach

Posting works, and the mechanism is worth understanding because it explains why most advice fails.

**The pipeline, in stages:**

1. **Quality filter** — spam, engagement-bait and artificial engagement are screened.
2. **Initial test** — shown to a small slice of your active network first.
3. **Scoring** — the response to that slice decides what happens next.
4. **Extended distribution** — strong posts spread by *topic interest*, beyond your network.

**Two signals dominate the scoring, and neither is likes:**

- **Dwell time** — how long people actually spend on the post. This is why a post someone reads
  properly beats one that collects fast reactions. **Write things worth stopping for**; that is the
  whole optimisation.
- **Comments, particularly substantive ones.** A real reply is a far stronger signal than a reaction,
  which is why "great post!" comment-pods do little.

**Distribution is interest-based, not just network-based.** LinkedIn reads what a post is *about* and
shows it to people interested in that topic, inside or outside your network. Two consequences:

- **Consistency on a topic compounds.** Posting repeatedly about one area builds recognised topical
  authority, so later posts reach the right audience faster. **Posting about everything builds none.**
- **Each post is a chance to gain followers who care about that specific topic** — which is the
  mechanism behind "thought leadership" actually working, when it does.

**What this means practically:** one substantial post a week on a consistent theme beats daily
fragments. Answer comments — it extends dwell and signals a live discussion. Write the first two
lines to earn the click, since everything after is hidden behind "see more".

**Engagement bait is filtered and it damages you.** So does an obviously AI-written post: recruiters
report perceiving AI authorship through their own heuristics far more often than through tools, and
the counter is specificity — details only you could have (`ai-hiring-funnel.md` §4).

## 4. Certifications — what they do and do not do

**Be precise about what a LinkedIn Learning certificate is: a certificate of completion.** There is
no proctored exam, no passing score and no external verification body. Adding one asserts that you
watched a course.

**So its value is real but narrow:**

| Does | Does not |
|---|---|
| Adds structured skill terms the search graph can match | Substitute for demonstrated experience |
| Shows deliberate direction — useful when pivoting | Carry weight as a credential with hiring managers |
| Fills a genuine gap in your own knowledge | Compensate for a thin experience section |

**Distinguish three tiers, because they are not comparable:**

1. **Proctored vendor and industry certifications** (cloud platforms, security, project management) —
   real assessment, real recognition, worth listing prominently.
2. **Course-completion certificates** (LinkedIn Learning and similar) — keyword and direction value.
3. **Attendance and webinar badges** — near zero.

**Recruiters weigh skills, experience and results first; certifications are secondary.** A profile
carrying twenty completion certificates and thin experience reads as someone who studies rather than
delivers — **the certificates actively work against you** at that ratio.

**The honest use:** take the course for the knowledge, list it if it is relevant and current, and
make sure something in your experience section shows you have *applied* it. **A certificate plus a
worked example is credible; a certificate alone is a claim.**

## 5. The edge nobody optimises: who is *in* your network is read as evidence

This is the least-exploited mechanic on the platform, and it works on humans and machines at once.

### Your network composition is treated as a statement about your level

**Recruiters and matching systems both infer seniority partly from the company you keep.** A profile
whose network is dense with directors, founders and recognised practitioners *in one field* reads as
someone operating in that field at that altitude. A profile with the same job history and a network
of 3,000 assorted strangers does not.

**Nobody writes this on a rubric, and everybody uses it** — it is the professional version of
judging a claim by who corroborates it. It is also why network *relevance* beats network *size* in a
way that is invisible if you only count connections.

**The actionable version: prune and concentrate.** A network built deliberately around one field, at
the level you are targeting, is a stronger signal than one twice the size and scattered. Most people
never curate at all.

### Shared connections are the trust transfer

When you surface in a recruiter's results, **mutual connections are displayed**. A 2nd-degree
result through someone the recruiter respects is a materially warmer object than a 3rd-degree
stranger — and it is the single most common route to an actual conversation.

**This means one well-chosen connection can be worth hundreds.** Being connected to the people who
*sit between you and your target market* — the recruiters, the community organisers, the visible
practitioners — changes how often you appear as "connected to someone I trust".

### Borrowed distribution — the genuinely underused tactic

**Connecting to a high-profile person does not give you their audience. Being *replied to* by one
does.**

The mechanism: comments are heavily weighted, and a comment on a widely-read post is shown to that
post's audience. So **a substantive comment on the right person's post reaches more of the right
people than a post to your own network ever will** — particularly early, when your own reach is
small.

**This inverts the usual advice.** People grind out original posts to 400 connections while ignoring
that thirty thoughtful comments on well-read posts in their field put them in front of thousands of
exactly the right readers — and in front of the author, who is often the person worth knowing.

Done properly it is not engagement farming: **add something the author did not say.** A correction, a
counter-example from your own experience, a "we tried this and here is what broke". Those get
replies from the author, which is the strongest version of the effect. "Great post!" gets nothing and
is visible as filler.

### Endorsements and recommendations are not equal

- **Endorsements from credible people in the field are worth more than volume.** The endorser's own
  standing in that skill is visible and read.
- **A written recommendation from a senior person is the highest-value social proof on the profile**
  and the most under-requested. It is specific, attributed and cannot be automated.
- **Ask while the work is fresh and while you still work together.** The request gets harder and less
  effective with every month, and impossible once you have both moved on.

### Where this backfires

- **A network of impressive strangers corroborates nothing.** If a recruiter mentions the mutual
  connection and that person does not know you, you have converted an advantage into a liability.
- **Never imply a relationship you do not have.** "I'm connected to X" is not "X knows my work", and
  in a small senior market the difference is one phone call away.
- **Proximity is not achievement.** Being adjacent to well-known people reads as a substitute for
  having done the work, unless your own evidence stands on its own.

**The honest formulation: a strong network makes real evidence travel further. It does not
manufacture evidence, and used that way it is quickly transparent.**

## 6. Marketing yourself into a target role

Where the goal is a specific role class — senior AI consultant, Head of AI, CTO — the mechanics above
compose:

- **Headline and skills carry the target, not only the history.** You are matched against searches for
  the role you want; if nothing in the structured fields relates to it, you do not appear.
- **Post on the target topic consistently** so topical distribution puts you in front of the people
  who hire for it, and so the association is established before you need it.
- **Write about judgement, not activity** — decisions, trade-offs, what broke and what it cost. That
  is what senior hiring tests for (`career-internal-visibility` §9), and it is the content that earns
  dwell time rather than reactions.
- **Public credibility must be corroborated.** A profile claiming AI leadership with no talks, posts,
  repositories or referees is a claim the first conversation will test.

**Do not manufacture a persona ahead of the evidence.** The gap closes in the interview, badly, and
in a small senior market it is remembered.

## 6. Anti-patterns

- **Believing an algorithm statistic** because many blogs repeat it.
- **Keyword-stuffing a semantic matcher.**
- **Leaving the skills section thin** — the only structured, filterable part of the profile.
- **Confusing the two open-to-work modes**, and telling your employer by accident.
- **Optimising connection count** rather than connection relevance.
- **Chasing likes** when dwell time and comments are what score.
- **Posting daily about everything**, defeating topical authority.
- **Engagement bait**, which is filtered.
- **Stacking completion certificates** against a thin experience section.
- **Listing a certificate with nothing in experience showing you applied it.**
- **Claiming a target-role persona** the first conversation will disprove.
